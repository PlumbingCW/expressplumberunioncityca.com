Signs a Sewer Line May Be Leaking

A sewer lateral is buried, so you rarely see the leak. You see what it does around the yard and the house.

Wet or lush patch

A soggy spot or unusually green strip over the lateral route, even in dry weather, can mean wastewater is escaping.

Sewer smell outside

A foul odor near the foundation or cleanout that comes and goes is a classic sign.

Slow drains everywhere

Several fixtures draining slowly together points to the main line, not a single trap.

Sunken ground

A dip in the lawn or driveway can mean the soil is washing away into a cracked pipe.

Gurgling and backups

Toilets gurgling when the washer runs, or water rising in the lowest drain, suggest a restriction or break.

Recurring roots

If you keep needing the line cleared, roots may have entered through a joint, which is a leak in itself.

None of these confirms a leak alone. A sewer camera inspection is how a plumber sees the pipe and the break.

Whose Pipe Is It in Union City?

Union Sanitary District serves sewer for Union City, Fremont and Newark. USD says each property owner is responsible for the entire sewer from the house to where it connects to the main, including the part under the sidewalk and street. That means a lateral leak is your repair, even when it sits beneath public ground.

If a backup starts, call USD's 24 hour line at (510) 477-7500. USD checks its public main for free, and if the main is clear, the blockage is probably in your lateral. That check tells you whether your next call goes to a plumber.

USD requires a repair permit before any lateral work, even on private property. Digging in the street also needs a city encroachment permit, and you must call 811 at least two working days before digging. A plumber who skips these steps is putting you at risk.

A sewer lateral repair follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) for pipe type, slope and joints, and the Union Sanitary District adds its own permit and inspection step. Ask who pulls the permit and who schedules the inspection before any digging starts.

Open Trench or Trenchless Lining

Open trench repair

  • Replaces the damaged section with new pipe
  • Best when the pipe has collapsed or has a belly
  • Needs excavation, so yards and driveways are disturbed
  • Lets the plumber correct slope as well

Trenchless lining

  • Lines the inside of the existing pipe, with little digging
  • Best for cracks, joints and root entry in intact pipe
  • Cannot fix a collapsed or badly misaligned section
  • Access points are still needed at both ends

The right choice depends on what the camera shows. If a plumber recommends a method without inspecting the pipe, ask why.

What to Do in the First Day

  1. Stop adding water

    Skip laundry, long showers and the dishwasher until the line is cleared. Every gallon you add has nowhere to go.

  2. Check the cleanout

    If you have a cleanout cap outdoors, look for standing water or smell, but do not open a cap on a backed-up line, because it can overflow. Older homes may lack one, which matters for the repair plan.

  3. Call USD for the main

    Ask USD to check the public main. Do not open manholes, which only USD personnel are authorized to do.

  4. Get a camera inspection

    A camera shows the break, its depth and the pipe material, which decides the repair and the permit path.

Before You Authorize Anything

Ask for the camera footage, the break location in feet from the cleanout and the permit plan in writing.
