Plumbing a Double Vanity in Union City, CA

A double vanity looks like two sinks and one cabinet. Behind the wall it is a drain, vent and supply layout that has to work together.

Two Sinks Means More Than Two Faucets

Homeowners often start with the cabinet. Plumbers start with the wall. A second sink needs its own trap, its own supply stops, and a drain that connects to a branch pipe sized and vented under code. If the wall is not opened and planned first, a double vanity can end up with sinks that gurgle and drain slowly.

This guide walks through the choices so you can talk to your plumber and contractor in the same language. For layout and installation work, see our plumbing installation page.

Three Layouts and What Each Asks of the Plumbing

1

Sinks centered on the old drain

Easiest when the existing drain sits near the middle. The branch drain splits to each sink, and the main supply moves little.

2

Sinks centered on the cabinet

The most common request. The drain and supply stubs shift sideways, which means opening the wall and rerouting pipe.

3

Moving the vanity to a new wall

A bigger job. New drain, vent and supply lines, and often concealed pipe replacement, which is treated as new work.

Shared Drain Branch or Separate Drains

Shared branch drain

  • Both traps tie into one properly sized branch
  • Less pipe and fewer wall penetrations
  • Needs correct venting so both sinks drain at once
  • Common choice in a typical remodel

Separate drains

  • Each sink runs its own line to the stack
  • More pipe and more wall or floor work
  • Can solve awkward layouts or distance problems
  • Costs more in labor and materials

Either layout can work. Your plumber should tell you which suits your wall, and why.

Rough-In Checklist Before the Wall Closes

  • ✓Confirm where the current drain and supplies exit, wall or floor.
  • ✓Mark the center of each sink on the finished plan.
  • ✓Plan two separate hot and cold stop valves, one pair per sink.
  • ✓Ask how each trap will be vented and where the branch connects.
  • ✓Replace old stops and supply lines while the wall is open.
  • ✓Photograph the open wall for future repairs.
  • ✓Test for leaks under pressure before closing the drywall.

A plumber who walks the wall with you, rather than quoting from a photo, is catching problems early.

What Happens on Rough-In Day

  1. Open the wall

    The plumber cuts back drywall to expose the existing drain, vent and supply pipes and checks their material and condition.

  2. Set the new layout

    Drain stubs and supply stubs are positioned to match the cabinet plan, with each trap arm kept within allowed distances of its vent.

  3. Pressure test

    The supply side is pressurized and the drain side is checked for slope and leaks before anything is covered.

  4. Schedule inspection if required

    If your job needs a permit, the inspector sees the pipe while the wall is still open. Closing early can mean cutting the drywall again.

  5. Finish after the cabinet

    Traps, faucets and stops go on once the top is set. Last comes a full flow test with both sinks running at once.

Cabinet Depth and Faucet Fit

Deep drawers and shallow cabinets collide with traps and supply stops. Check the cabinet plans against the drain location before you buy. A faucet that fits one sink and clashes with the other is common on shared tops, so confirm the hole spacing.

Also plan the counter. A top with two pre-drilled faucet holes and a wide spread suits some faucets and not others, and an undermount sink changes where the drain lands. Settle the top, the sinks and the faucets as a set before the plumber sets the rough-in.
